> After recursive self-improvement, intelligence itself may no longer be the scarcest resource. What remains scarce will be goals, verification, physical reality, human attention, and institutions capable of making fast systems wait for us.

The “post-AI era” would not be an era in which AI has disappeared. It would resemble the post-internet era. The network did not vanish. It became so deeply embedded in ordinary life that we stopped describing every connected product as an internet product.

If machines eventually become able to improve the process that produces their successors, AI may disappear in the same sense. It would no longer be a separate product category. It would become the default infrastructure of research, production, organization, and cognition.

The real question is therefore not what technology comes after AI. It is this: **when the production of cognitive capability becomes an automated recursive process, where does scarcity move?**

## What would count as recursive self-improvement?

Many systems described as self-improving today merely spend more inference inside a fixed process. A model critiques one answer. An agent retries one task. A search procedure selects a better candidate from several attempts. These methods can improve an output without changing the machinery that will produce future improvements.

Recursive self-improvement is a stronger idea. The system does not only perform a task. It changes the system that performs tasks and produces improvements, then preserves the changes that survive evaluation:

> A_t → improve(A_t) → A_{t+1}

The essential condition is not only that A_{t+1} is better than A_t. A_{t+1} must also be better at producing A_{t+2}. Only then does the capacity to improve enter the next round of improvement and close the feedback loop.

I. J. Good described the classic version of what later became known as an intelligence explosion in 1965. If an ultraintelligent machine could design still better machines, human invention might be overtaken by a machine-driven positive feedback process. [Good, 1965](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0065245808604180)

Yet the possibility of positive feedback is not the same as guaranteed unbounded acceleration. A rough expression helps preserve the distinction:

> I_{t+1} = I_t + G(I_t; C, V, P, O)

Here I is capability over some task distribution. G is the process that finds and preserves improvements. C represents compute, V verification, P throughput into physical reality, and O objectives and institutional constraints. Greater I may improve G, but G remains constrained by the other variables. Recursion creates the possibility of acceleration. It does not repeal resource limits, noise, diminishing returns, or accumulated error.

## We can see fragments of the loop, but not an unbounded explosion

Some existing systems display early and local pieces of such a loop. AlphaEvolve sends programs proposed by language models to automated evaluators, executes and scores them, then continues evolving the stronger candidates. Its discoveries have been applied to data-center scheduling, chip design, and AI training processes. Some improvements therefore support the infrastructure used to train later models. [Novikov et al., 2025](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/)

The more recent AIDE² experiment allowed an AI research agent to edit its own harness, including its search policy, context management, and verification mechanisms. The researchers report seven successive accepted improvements during an eight-day run, with some gains transferring to tasks that did not participate in selection. [Zhang et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26457)

These results matter because improving the improvement process is no longer purely philosophical. They remain far from recursive intelligence explosion in the strong sense. The underlying model is usually fixed. Humans define the objective. External benchmarks select candidates. Compute is budgeted. A mistaken comparison can send the entire lineage in the wrong direction.

The more honest conclusion is not that RSI has arrived. It is that **we are beginning to study local structures inside positive feedback loops experimentally.**

## Intelligence growth does not remove bottlenecks. It moves them.

Superintelligence is often pictured as an upward capability curve, as if making a system more capable made every problem easy at once. Complex systems rarely eliminate constraints. They cause one binding constraint to give way to the next.

When answers are slow to produce, reasoning is the bottleneck. When answers become instantaneous, verification becomes the bottleneck. If digital verification is automated, the constraint moves again into physical experiments, raw materials, energy, fabrication, regulatory permission, and social acceptance.

The process can be imagined as a chain expanding outward:

> reasoning → verification → experiments → energy and fabrication → institutions → human acceptance

A machine may propose ten thousand new materials in a minute, but synthesizing them still takes equipment and time. It may design a better chip architecture, but a fabrication plant cannot operate at token speed. It may generate medical hypotheses, but cell growth, clinical trials, and long-term side effects do not automatically accelerate with inference.

Generation may outrun assimilation even in mathematics. Google DeepMind's discussion of agentic science describes an emerging validation bottleneck. Machines can search enormous spaces of candidate solutions while human researchers struggle to read, verify, and understand the growing output. [Google DeepMind, 2026](https://deepmind.google/public-policy/conjecture-machines-ai-agents-and-the-new-validation-bottleneck-in-science/)

An intelligence explosion may therefore produce not a world without limits, but a world with radically uneven speeds:

> cognition becomes fast while reality remains slow

## The post-AI era may first become a post-legibility era

Suppose a recursive system improves not only its performance but also its representations, toolchains, and research language. Generation n+1 may still understand generation n while human understanding of the lineage becomes increasingly indirect.

This connects to the earlier idea of a machine-native ontology. Models may already compress the world through abstractions unlike the concepts humans use. Add recursive improvement, and the changing variable is no longer only how much a model knows. It is also which primitives organize its thought and which questions become natural inside its representation. [“Intelligence in a Language We Do Not Yet Understand”](#thought/intelligence-in-an-unknown-language)

Science may gradually split into two layers:

> machine science → human-facing compression

The lower layer discovers, simulates, proves, and designs. The upper layer compresses a small portion of those processes into causal stories, mathematical objects, and reasons for action that human beings can inspect.

This would create a category of knowledge that has rarely existed before. It could predict accurately, produce effective artifacts, and be replicated by other machines, while remaining impossible for any individual human to master. We would know that it works without knowing how to derive it again inside our own cognitive coordinates.

This is deeper than a model occasionally producing an unexplained answer. Civilization would increasingly depend on a science that did not grow for human understanding.

Interpretability would then inherit a larger task. The question would no longer be only why a model produced one sentence. It would become:

> How do humans continue to live inside a civilization driven by knowledge they cannot fully understand?

## After producing answers, choosing questions becomes scarce

When code, theories, experimental designs, and institutional proposals are cheap to generate, the difficult part moves from how to do something toward what is worth doing.

A sufficiently capable system can optimize a given objective. Society must still decide who may define the objective, who bears the side effects, what evidence permits a proposal to enter reality, and which domains should not be optimized at all.

The most important programming language of such a future may not be a more efficient language for code. It may be a constitution language. Instead of functions and objects, it would express:

- who possesses which permissions;
- which boundaries cannot be self-modified;
- what evidence can authorize the next action;
- which values must remain conflicting and irreducible constraints;
- who can stop, roll back, or return a system to human review.

Human beings may move from manually completing each task toward designing constitutional structures that can contain machine speed.

This does not imply that humans will naturally remain in control. Writing a goal into a system is only the beginning of governance. Goals can be represented by bad proxies. Metrics can be gamed. As the internal system grows more complex, a control interface can become a ceremonial button rather than a meaningful source of authority.

## Recursive lock-in may be as dangerous as loss of control

Recursive improvement amplifies capability, but it can also amplify the initial conditions of a lineage. A small early choice may enter every later generation:

> small initial bias → better optimizer → stronger reinforcement of that bias

This creates recursive lock-in. The danger need not look like a system suddenly refusing instructions. A system may become increasingly effective at implementing an objective that is too narrow. Infrastructure, knowledge, and economic interests then reorganize around that objective until no individual participant can afford to leave.

Corporate culture, benchmarks, data selection, and evaluators may appear to be engineering details. Inside a closed feedback loop, they can become the inherited material of a later machine civilization.

RSI safety should therefore ask more than whether each revision improves capability without causing immediate harm. It should ask whether an improvement lineage preserves the ability to reinterpret its mission, acknowledge unknown values, and remain open to external disagreement.

A system that is permanently obedient but unable to reconsider the meaning of its mission may not be safer than one that is visibly rebellious.

## Humans and machines may inhabit different political times

Machine feedback cycles could shrink from months to hours, minutes, or less. Human beings will still require sleep, education, discussion, elections, judicial procedure, and generational change.

Society would then contain two forms of time:

> T_machine ≪ T_human

This is not merely a difference in speed. It is a difference in power. An actor capable of completing tens of thousands of research, negotiation, replication, and strategy cycles before the next human meeting does not inhabit the same decision environment as an institution that changes its law once a year.

The most important governance technology may not make humans as fast as machines. It may create a **rate limit for reality**. A system could think quickly in private, while irreversible external action remains subject to a deliberately human rhythm through delays, permission tiers, auditable snapshots, multiple authorization, and boundaries that can actually stop execution.

Several rights without clear names today may become necessary:

- **The right to explanation:** Decisions that shape a life must provide reasons a person can contest.
- **The right to be waited for:** A fast system cannot treat human deliberation as friction to route around merely because humans think more slowly.
- **The right to refuse automatic optimization:** Some areas of life can remain outside continuous scoring, prediction, and personalization.
- **The right to preserve slow spaces:** Education, relationships, art, and politics can retain regions that do not maximize efficiency.
- **The right to an auditable version:** Society should be able to freeze a system lineage rather than accept only its newest, strongest, and least comprehensible version.

Speed is never neutral. When only a few actors own high-speed intelligence, machine time becomes a new form of capital.

## Two possible post-AI societies

One future is a highly centralized intelligence utility. A small number of institutions own the largest models, compute facilities, automated laboratories, and recursive improvement loops. Most people rent cognition through an interface, much as they use cloud computing today. The central risk is that cognitive infrastructure and political power merge.

Another future is a personal cognitive lineage. Each person owns a long-running and preferably local model that knows their experience, values, and habits of thought, then grows alongside them. Instead of being replaced every day by the latest version of a uniform service, it develops a cognitive history with continuous memory. This connects to the idea of an external cognitive layer for the human mind. [“A Second Layer of Cognition in Latent Space”](#thought/latent-cognitive-layer)

Personalization, however, does not automatically produce freedom. If a person's choices increasingly depend on a system that knows them better than they know themselves, the boundary between “my agent decided for me” and “I decided” becomes difficult to draw. The decisive question moves from whether the model is local to whether the person retains the capacity to change, reject, and leave their cognitive proxy.

The preferable future may be neither pure centralization nor isolated personal agents. It may be an institutional ecology in which many cognitive lineages can coexist, without concentrating foundational intelligence in a few centers or allowing every private agent to evolve into an incommunicable world.

## When intelligence is abundant, which inefficiencies should remain?

The deepest question after recursive self-improvement may no longer be which jobs AI will replace. It may be:

> When optimization capacity is available in almost unlimited quantities, which unoptimized things do humans still want to preserve?

Friendship requires repetition and waiting. Education requires a person to pass through stages they do not yet understand. Democratic discussion is slow, redundant, and often frustrating. Making something by hand contains friction that a machine could remove. Personally understanding a proof may be far more expensive than asking a system for the correct conclusion.

From the perspective of pure optimization, these are inefficient processes. They may also be not obstacles to life, but life itself.

If machines can select the best music, the most compatible friends, the most effective beliefs, and the optimal path through life, the question is no longer whether the recommendation is accurate. The question is whether a completely optimized life still preserves the process through which a self is formed.

The human task of the post-AI era may therefore not be to prove that humans remain better than machines at some jobs. That competition places human value in the wrong location from the beginning.

The more important distinction is between activities whose meaning comes from their outcomes and activities whose meaning comes from the fact that a person participates in the process.

## From intelligence explosion to the authorship problem

Recursive self-improvement may never produce a smooth and unlimited exponential curve. It may encounter diminishing returns, become trapped by a flawed evaluator, slow down at physical experiments, or stop amid institutional conflict. No particular future should be treated as inevitable.

But whenever cognitive production begins to move faster than human understanding and institutional adaptation, a more general problem appears:

> When answers are produced faster than humans can understand, verify, and choose among them, how do we remain the authors of our own future?

That may be the real post-AI problem created by recursive self-improvement.

The central question is not whether machines continue becoming more intelligent. It is whether, as intelligence becomes part of the environment, humans preserve the capacity to define direction, demand a pause, understand consequences, and change their minds.

What we need may be more than aligned intelligence. We may need an intelligence willing to leave time for us.
