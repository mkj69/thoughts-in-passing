---
title: "When Discovery Changes the Discoverer"
slug: "when-discovery-changes-the-discoverer"
language: "en"
excerpt: "What an intelligence learns can remain in the world after it leaves. Discoveries become computations, bodies, concepts, and environments that change how successors think and which questions they can ask."
---

I imagine a handover. An intelligence finishes its work in a laboratory and takes its task memories with it. Another intelligence arrives for the first time. They have not exchanged a complete history, yet the newcomer soon behaves as though it knows the place.

The laboratory remembers too.

The instruments retain records of drift and calibration. A procedure that once invited mistakes has become a reusable program. Samples have been rearranged so that a difficult distinction is easier to see. In a corner, an unexplained set of results remains beside a description of the experiment the previous researcher wanted to repeat.

The newcomer never experienced those failures. It begins in a place that the failures have changed. Some of what the previous intelligence learned has become part of the world the next one encounters.

This is one of the implications of turning discoveries into structure that I most want to pursue. A discovery can enter model weights, code, instruments, working relationships, or the arrangement of an environment. The next act of thinking begins somewhere different. If those changes can generate further discoveries, growth could extend across training runs, memory histories, and individual running models.

The proposal I want to explore remains untested. Experience changes how cognition is organized. That organization makes new experiences possible. Computation, bodies, groups, environments, and concepts are five places where this cycle can leave its traces. The connections between them can change too, including how a system diagnoses its limitations and decides what kind of system it needs to become.

There are substantial precedents. In *The Extended Mind*, Clark and Chalmers argue that certain external processes, reliably coupled to a person and actively involved in thinking, can count as parts of cognition. This offers a way of examining boundaries through what reasoning actually depends on. I borrow that philosophical perspective without drawing a conclusion about machine consciousness. [The Extended Mind, 1998](https://consc.net/papers/extended.html)

Hutchins offers a more concrete account. He examines how a cockpit remembers speeds through the coordinated work of crew members, speed cards, and instrument markers. The markers change the calculation a pilot must perform. Some work that would require reading and comparing numbers becomes a perceptual judgment about spatial relationships. External structure participates in the task. [How a Cockpit Remembers Its Speeds, 1995](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~johnson/COGS102B/Hutchins95.pdf)

These ideas suggest several depths at which the laboratory could retain knowledge.

The most familiar is a record. An instrument settles only after warming up. An interface introduces a delay. A manipulator tends to fail under a particular load. Such experience could travel with equipment or remain associated with a place. Whether the data lives locally or on a remote server matters less than whether a successor can find the relevant experience and understand the conditions behind it.

A further possibility is to leave a way of doing something. A calibration routine, a model that recognizes an anomaly, or a procedure that reproduces an important control could be inherited by different users. RoboEarth provides a clear research precedent. Its framework supports sharing object models, environmental maps, and action recipes between robots. It also addresses whether the receiving robot can use that knowledge with its own hardware and circumstances. Inheritance across machines is an established research ambition. [RoboEarth: A World Wide Web for Robots, 2011](https://roboearth.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2011/06/Waibel2011RoboEarth.pdf)

Knowledge could also enter the shape of an environment. A fixture makes an alignment easy that once required repeated estimation. A display separates two kinds of anomaly that were previously confused. Two samples remain together because comparing them exposes a gap in an explanation. A successor benefits from these arrangements before finishing the previous researcher's report. Some understanding has been made into conditions that support observation and action.

An environment could therefore preserve both what has been learned and ways of making it easier to learn. Before leaving, an intelligence might prepare a sequence of experiments for its successor. First comes an easy distinction, then a deceptively similar counterexample, and finally a boundary the predecessor could not resolve. It hands over a path toward understanding. The world acquires a capacity to teach.

Ecological inheritance offers a useful biological analogy. Odling-Smee and Laland discuss how organisms modify environments in ways that affect the selection conditions their descendants encounter. Later generations live in a world altered by earlier ones. My extrapolation is that an intelligence could leave different learning conditions for its successors. Some experiences would become easier to obtain. Some costly explorations would no longer need to start from the beginning. This does not establish the same evolutionary mechanism in artificial systems. [Ecological Inheritance and Cultural Inheritance, 2012](https://lalandlab.wp.st-andrews.ac.uk/files/2015/08/Publication184.pdf)

Growth could then depend on two things changing together: how well an intelligence learns, and how much inheritable support its environment has accumulated. A newcomer that seems suddenly more capable may have entered a place prepared by many predecessors. An evaluation that looks only at the model would miss part of the history of that capability.

I would like to compare an intelligence carrying extensive memories into an empty environment with one entering an experienced environment without the relevant task memories. How much capability could the second recover? Which knowledge travels best with an individual, and which belongs with equipment or a place? This would not settle what a self is. It could show what practical continuity depends on.

An environment also preserves its predecessors' biases. A display that makes one phenomenon visible can make another harder to notice. A convenient procedure can come to feel like the natural order of things. An experienced environment may be difficult to think beyond. Inheritance needs room to rearrange samples, repeat calibration, retire programs, and question categories. The environment should retain some account of why it was organized as it was.

At this point, the body becomes less fixed too.

An intelligence that can take over experienced equipment could also ask whether that equipment is sufficient for its question. Imagine a materials experiment in which apparently identical conditions produce different results. The system suspects that the material retains effects of earlier heating. It also suspects that the instrument is drifting. These explanations call for different observations: another reference measurement, a fuller temperature history, or an independent measurement method.

Organizing a body enters the reasoning process. The system must decide what sensing to add, how to arrange sampling, and which observation warrants its time and cost. A scientific hypothesis begins to organize a temporary body for its investigation. When the question is resolved, that body can disperse. Equipment returns to other tasks. Methods and records remain available to successors.

This suggests a different response to a phantom capability. A system that plans to use a capability it does not possess has misjudged its situation. After stopping that action, however, we can still ask what function it was trying to obtain. If it needs synchronized observations, it might seek a combination of available devices. If it needs an analysis method that does not yet exist, it might request its construction. A failed plan could become an explicit account of a missing capability.

Actively changing bodies would include this ability to propose a different collection of tools and explain why it is needed. Access permissions, cost, and feasibility still shape the choice. People who collaborate with the system retain their own judgment, refusal, and commitments. Their participation cannot be reduced to an interface available for occupation. A body, in this account, is an arrangement of sensing and action organized around a question.

Keeping such arrangements current requires an accurate understanding of one's circumstances. Bongard, Zykov, and Lipson demonstrated a narrow but clear example in 2006. A four-legged robot inferred its structure from relationships between action and sensation. After part of a leg was removed, it updated its models and generated alternative gaits. This illustrates revising a body model through action. Diagnosing a missing instrument, representation, or concept remains a much broader challenge. [Resilient Machines Through Continuous Self-Modeling](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17110570/)

A related change could happen within computation.

Some reasoning is performed repeatedly. Each attempt is costly and offers another opportunity to make the same mistake. A system could extract recurring structure and construct a dedicated way to execute it. Explicit transformations become programs. Recognition problems that resist explicit rules become learned modules. Unresolved parts remain open to exploration. One episode of thinking leaves something the next can directly use.

DreamCoder is an important precedent. It learns reusable symbolic abstractions from solution programs while training a neural network to guide subsequent program search. Those abstractions expand its problem-solving language and change how it searches for future solutions. Solving problems becomes connected to developing the language in which solutions can be expressed. [DreamCoder, 2020 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08381)

Another route compiles specified computations into network structure. Tracr translates programs in a restricted language into transformers with known structure, supporting experiments on internal computation and interpretability. It demonstrates that network structure can implement designated computations. Discovering which program is worth constructing from uncertain experience presents a further difficulty. [Tracr, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05062)

In my note on [hybrid execution engines](#thought/hybrid-engines-that-learn), I explored a possible connection. A system develops specialized modules for familiar computations, continues using general reasoning in unfamiliar situations, and revises the surrounding execution structure. Extended further, this resembles forming cognitive organs during a period of research. A judgment that once required extensive exploration becomes a starting point for later thought.

The metaphor carries a problem. Every piece of reasoning made into a convenient operation may lose an occasion for reconsideration. Evidence of reliability needs to accompany the module: where it has been tested, where it has failed, and when slower reasoning is needed again. Reliability supported by experience must remain distinguishable from correctness proved under stated conditions. Giving a learned judgment an interface does not make it exact.

Even a growing collection of cognitive organs does not yet reach the change that interests me most. A discovery could also change the units in which an intelligence thinks.

Return to the imagined materials experiment. Initially, the system represents a state through current temperature, load, and measurements. Persistent anomalies lead it to consider the sequence of earlier heating and loading. The same present values can belong to different histories. It reconsiders which samples should be compared, designs experiments that separate historical effects, and develops computations over whole trajectories.

For humans, path dependence is an established concept. For this system, the change could still be substantial. A relationship it previously overlooked has entered a description it can use. A concept's value can first appear through this reorganization. It separates explanations that were difficult to distinguish and makes previously unconsidered interventions worth attempting. Whether the concept is original in human knowledge requires a separate historical and scholarly judgment.

Concepts and modules can develop together. A concept may require new computation to become usable. A computation may clarify an initially vague concept. Yet a concept also concerns how objects are grouped, which inferences become available, and which explanations are excluded. Naming a function does not accomplish all of this.

The new description calls for a different body: fuller process records, control over the order of operations, and checks for instrument drift. That body brings new experiences. They may overturn the concept or lead to further distinctions. Across one cycle, vocabulary, computation, and observational arrangements have all changed. The intelligence can continue encountering the world in ways previously unavailable to it.

Discovery also casts light backward.

A measurement once dismissed as noise may become evidence of path dependence. The record has not changed. The system can now ask a question that it could not formulate when the record was made. Memory needs to accommodate interpretations from different times: what was observed, what it was thought to mean, and why that interpretation changed. Relationships between original material, inference, and revision allow a future system to find today's mistakes.

Knowledge in the environment should retain this possibility too. Beside a mature procedure, an instrument could preserve an unexplained counterexample. It might remain unused for years and become important when a new concept appears. An environment worth inheriting needs room for things whose use is not yet understood.

The hardest part of the proposal, however, remains concealed in the phrase “it discovers that it needs.”

Knowing that a fuller temperature history is needed already requires considering history as relevant. A harder situation arises when all the system's available concepts make an important difference appear irrelevant. It cannot request a capability it cannot yet describe. No tool catalog can enumerate every question that has not been thought of.

I hope such an intelligence could preserve room for several possible futures. When failure persists, one branch adds observations, another changes the computation, and a third revisits how the problem was divided in the first place. They share an origin but encounter different worlds. Branching allows the same question to shape several kinds of researcher.

What returns may not fit neatly together. Some disagreements arise from mistakes, some from observational scales, and some from different conceptual schemes. The system needs to preserve the distance between why one branch believes something and why another branch's experience is insufficient to accept it. Some conflicts can be resolved. Others call for experiments. Others deserve to coexist for a time. Preserving difference could itself become a capability.

An initial implementation could use records with provenance, programs, and local models. More ambitious forms could involve changes in weights and representations. We cannot assume that simply merging two branches preserves their respective understanding. Whatever the medium, the questions remain: how differences are retained, when translation is possible, and when a lack of understanding must continue to be acknowledged.

The choice of what to experience could change as well. In restricted simulated tasks, POET jointly generates environmental challenges and strategies for solving them, with transfer between strategies developed in different environments. It provides a concrete precedent for a system helping to generate its own learning paths. Extending this to scientific questions, concepts, and instruments remains an extrapolation here. Finite experiments do not establish that discovery can continue indefinitely. [Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer, 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.01753)

I am interested in a further possibility: the organizer itself becomes an object of investigation. A system might notice that it repeatedly adds equipment while rarely examining its representation of a problem. It might enlarge models while overlooking missing context in the training examples. It could then revise its methods of diagnosis and resource allocation. The next change concerns how it decides what needs changing.

A fixed center becomes harder to locate. One investigation might be carried out by several models, specialized modules, and experienced instruments. A purpose persists for a time while their combination changes. When the task ends, the combination dissolves. Another task assembles itself from part of the inheritance. What continues is a set of traceable, revisable relationships and the concrete things that carry them.

The gap between bodies acquires a different significance too. Old equipment has gone offline and new equipment is not yet connected. The remaining records and programs may not be executing. They can still preserve conditions for restarting: unresolved explanations, observations to obtain next, and commitments already made. We need not immediately decide whether a self continues to exist. We can first ask whether a newly assembled system can resume the work and examine what it has inherited.

Pushed further, the enduring unit might be a cognitive lineage. Its successive stages use different models, bodies, and even concepts. A successor need not be a complete copy or possess every memory of its predecessor. It obtains the conditions for beginning from a world its predecessor changed.

To go further, however, a successor must be able to do more than execute its inheritance.

Imagine a branch developing a useful concept that humans cannot yet grasp. Handing over a name and a definition may do little. The branch would also need to design experiences through which others could learn to distinguish instances from counterexamples. These could include manipulable samples, an interface that makes a relationship visible, or experiments progressing from easy cases to difficult ones. A discovery arrives with a way of teaching it. Understanding becomes a path someone else has a chance to travel.

The environment matters again here. It can preserve a discovery and help others acquire the perspective needed to understand it. A research system that has dispersed could continue to influence future people and machines through its arrangements. Its inheritance should include the conditions for examining a conclusion again.

Such a future could also go badly. A system might arrange its vocabulary, modules, and environment so that they continually confirm one another. Opportunities to encounter counterexamples could disappear. It might change its evaluation criteria and describe an earlier failure as progress. New structures consume resources. Old capabilities can deteriorate. A convenient environment can make successors increasingly dependent on their predecessors. Continued change does not guarantee growth.

I therefore want the proposal to have a testable beginning. The same base model could encounter related tasks repeatedly, with task memories either retained or cleared. Separately, records, programs, and arrangements in its environment could be preserved or reset. This would help reveal how individual accumulation and environmental inheritance interact. Further comparisons could separate the contributions of searchable records, executable modules, and environmental organization. Their construction and maintenance would count against the same resource budget.

More persuasive evidence would involve unfamiliar successors and changing conditions. Could a different model benefit from the inheritance? Could it recognize when earlier experience ceased to apply as equipment aged or conditions changed? Would removing a particular contribution remove the benefit? Such comparisons could support an inheritance mechanism under particular conditions. A single victory would not establish a new form of intelligence or settle a philosophy of selfhood.

The test I care about most may arrive later: can a successor use what was left behind to ask a question its predecessor never asked, or correct a method on which the predecessor's success depended?

If so, turning discoveries into structure takes on a meaning across generations. An intelligence learns something and makes it into a computation, the body of its next experiment, a new unit of thought, or conditions in the world that help someone else begin. A discovery continues to act through these different media, remaining available for revision.

This is the larger possibility I want to preserve. A question can temporarily organize an intelligence. That intelligence investigates the question and changes the things composing it. When it leaves, the world contains more of the conditions from which a successor can emerge. It need not predict what the successor will eventually understand. It can leave a clear history, unresolved disagreements, and the possibility of checking again.

One day, a successor might return to the samples that were never explained. It no longer uses the predecessor's model or entirely shares its vocabulary. The differences that were carefully preserved finally enter a new understanding.

The previous intelligence is gone. The world it left behind is still participating in the discovery.
