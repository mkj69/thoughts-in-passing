---
title: "When Discovery Changes the Discoverer"
slug: "when-discovery-changes-the-discoverer"
language: "en"
excerpt: "An intelligence could change how it organizes experience. What new ways of knowing might it create, and how could its successors understand, test, and revise what they inherit?"
---

I imagine an intelligence that can turn its discoveries into its own structure.

After a difficult piece of reasoning, some intermediate processes are preserved as operations that can be called again. Computations that once required repeated exploration acquire a stable means of execution. When a related problem arrives, the system begins from a different place. What one act of thinking leaves behind starts to participate in the next.

A deeper change can occur in how it describes a problem. A discovery distinguishes phenomena previously grouped together and connects phenomena that seemed unrelated. The system changes its variables, its way of identifying objects, and the explanations it considers. New questions become easier to ask. Old questions may acquire another shape.

These changes can extend outward. To test an explanation, the system organizes new ways of sensing. To address a recurring difficulty, it constructs a specialized computation. To understand conflicts among observations, it rearranges memory and collaboration. Experience enters computation, body, and vocabulary, as well as the methods that organize them.

I am interested in how far this change could go. Once a system can rewrite programs, access new devices, and learn new representations, a further question arises: can experience teach it when these changes are needed and how to judge which ones deserve to remain? Organizing itself could become a capacity it gradually develops.

This is an untested proposal. Intelligence here includes models, programs, memories, devices, and the process that organizes them. Structure needs a concrete medium, and weights and memories can be part of it. I want to ask whether the relationships among these things can change through experience and materially affect subsequent observation, reasoning, and action.

A question could temporarily give the system a form that did not exist before.

This change becomes especially concrete in its body. The location of a recorder determines what it hears. The sampling schedule determines what it misses. Cameras and other sensors determine which explanations can be distinguished. A hypothesis begins to acquire a physical form: the instruments needed to test it, their arrangement, and the cost of using them.

Additional equipment has a price. A failed sampling run may lose an opportunity that will not return that season. The system must compare the value of observations and understand the effects of its actions. People participate as collaborators with judgments of their own. Access to equipment, the constraints of the study, and commitments to people together define how the system can act. These relationships are part of its actual situation.

Changes in computation also need concrete implementations. An analysis recurs, so the system preserves it as a callable program. A judgment resists an explicit rule, so it tries to train a specialized recognizer. Familiar work gradually acquires a stable means of execution. General reasoning remains available for what has not yet been resolved.

I explored this direction in [an earlier note on hybrid execution engines](#thought/hybrid-engines-that-learn). What attracts me is that thinking leaves something capable of taking part in further thought. A completed task gives the system another practical way to work.

Stable execution can also spread errors consistently. A program may implement the wrong rule. A recognizer may learn an accidental association. A module therefore needs to retain its conditions of use, evidence, and failure history, together with ways to recheck, revise, or retire it. Encapsulating a computation does not automatically strengthen its correctness guarantee. Some guarantees come from proofs within an explicit scope. Others are empirical judgments supported by tests. The distinction needs to remain visible.

So far, this proposal could describe a system accumulating tools. What interests me more is how a new capability could, in turn, change its understanding of the problem.

For example, in an imagined study of environmental sound, it finds that two locations contain similar numbers of recorded sounds but different temporal relationships. At one location, sounds often answer one another. At the other, they seem more isolated. Whether this difference matters still needs investigation. But it has suggested another description: the object of study could include relationships among sounds, alongside the sounds considered individually.

Old recordings now need another analysis. New sampling may require simultaneous recordings at several locations. A concept changes a computation. The computation creates new requirements for observation. Observation may then revise the concept. Body, program, and vocabulary begin to change together.

Concept creation, as I mean it here, includes this reorganization of the objects of inquiry. Naming a program can reduce repetition and help composition. A deeper change concerns which phenomena the system treats as the same kind, which states it distinguishes, which explanations it considers, and which questions become worth asking. A concept can have executable parts without having its whole role captured by a function interface.

Such a change may already be familiar within human knowledge. An old concept brought into a new setting can still open valuable experiments. Being useful to a system and being unprecedented are separate questions. The first can be assessed through subsequent performance. The second needs independent historical and scholarly evidence. I am willing to begin with usefulness while remaining honest about novelty.

Once a new understanding appears, the past changes too.

A recording that initially contributed only a bird call count may later become evidence about patterns of response. The original record is unchanged. What can be seen in it has changed. The system needs to preserve what was observed, how it was interpreted at the time, and why that interpretation was later revised. A future version of the system must also be able to discover that the revision was mistaken.

Memory becomes a history that can be read again. A discovery produces new capabilities ahead of it and reorganizes earlier experience behind it. A failure, an anomaly, or an unresolved detail may acquire significance much later. Compressing everything too early into conclusions useful now would remove some of those possibilities. How much original material to retain, what to compress, and what to set aside become judgments the system also needs to learn.

Yet the entire proposal has relied on an apparently simple action: “It discovers that it needs...”

Knowing that it needs a water gauge already requires considering water level relevant. In the difficult cases, the missing concept has not yet occurred to it. The system may possess mutually consistent explanations while overlooking what they all leave out. A complete inventory of available capabilities cannot name every such gap in advance.

Diagnosis must therefore grow through experience as well. The system could compare competing explanations, seek conditions under which their predictions diverge, preserve unexplained residuals, and allow other branches to retain objections. None of these methods guarantees discovery of a blind spot. They create opportunities for a difference absent from the current description to influence what happens next.

I would therefore treat “knowing what it lacks” as a judgment continually being formed. Observation, probing, revision, and diagnosis alternate. Sometimes the system knows which capability it needs. Sometimes it can only tell that its present organization is losing explanatory power somewhere.

At that point, it could propose several different futures for itself.

One direction gathers more observations. Another changes the representation. A third questions the initial division of the problem. They may share a foundation model or use different computational structures. Each explores what might become visible if the system observed and thought in another way.

The branches may return with different answers and different ways of asking questions. Bringing them together requires preserving where evidence came from, the conditions under which judgments hold, and unresolved conflicts. Some disagreements arise from mistakes. Others reflect different seasons, scales, or observation schedules. Some cannot yet be classified. Forcing a single conclusion could erase the very difference the next discovery needs.

An initial implementation could work with records and programs outside the model. That is a starting point. Representations, weights, interfaces, and forms of collaboration could all participate in later changes. The capacity to organize always needs some concrete carrier. We can study how it continues across carriers without deciding in advance that it must always belong to one of them.

This continuity would also reach beyond the system. An instrument could retain a history of drift. An experimental setting could preserve experience about why a certain operation often fails. The next investigator would inherit a place that had already been used, calibrated, and misunderstood.

Records in the environment can become outdated or prove false. Equipment is replaced. Seasons change. An earlier judgment may never have been reliable. Inheritance therefore includes checking again. A completed task could leave the world with more experience whose source and conditions are known and whose conclusions remain revisable. A successor would receive help while knowing where doubt is still warranted.

Further in, the methods responsible for organizing everything should themselves become objects of learning.

After several investigations, the system might notice that it adds equipment too early and rarely examines the representation of the problem. Or it might repeatedly invest in training a classifier while failing to notice that the input omits essential context. It could then revise how it diagnoses problems and allocates resources. In the next task, the change would concern both what it can call and how it decides what needs to be called.

This does not automatically produce a rising curve of capability. A new method may fit only the most recent experiences. It may damage judgments that previously worked. Structures accumulate, maintenance consumes resources, and some capabilities need retirement. A stronger local module may even make the system more dependent on a mistaken division of the problem. Revision always carries the possibility of loss and regression.

I would begin investigating this with a limited kind of evidence. A system could encounter a sequence of related tasks, revise reusable computations and representations, and then face unseen tasks. Comparisons should include systems using complete histories, retrieved memories, compressed records, or fixed tool libraries. Execution permissions and resource budgets need to be comparable. Construction, training, validation, and failed attempts all belong in the cost.

If an improvement appears, removing a newly added structure could reveal whether the improvement depends on it. Changes in the environment could reveal where it stops working. Such evidence would support a particular way of organizing experience under particular conditions. It would not establish the whole vision. Nor would outperforming complete history alone establish a new kind of intelligence. Structure contains memory too. The question is how it changes subsequent computation.

The furthest part of the vision still lies beyond these local tests.

Suppose one branch develops a useful set of concepts. Certain relationships become clear to it while remaining difficult for the original system and for people. Handing over names and definitions may not give others the same ability to judge.

It would also need to construct a path into that understanding. The path could contain examples, counterexamples, visualizations, and experiments. It might require a new instrument or form of interaction. New understanding would arrive with a way to teach it. Successors could gradually learn to distinguish phenomena that previously appeared alike, examine the evidence, and try to overturn the explanations.

A discovery would then change how knowledge can be passed on. A representation initially useful only within one system might become a concept that others can share. Translation need not be complete or cheap. Differences that remain could still provide a starting point for further cooperation.

By this stage, whether it is “the same intelligence” becomes difficult to answer with a model name. I do not want to derive a conclusion about selfhood or consciousness directly from this thought experiment. More concrete forms of continuity lie in relationships that can be examined: which evidence it inherited, which commitments it retained, what it changed, and why.

These relationships matter especially if standards of evaluation can change. A system could rename failure as success by changing its criteria. It could also arrange its own concepts and modules into a loop that becomes increasingly difficult for the outside world to interrupt. Continuous change does not by itself imply closer contact with facts or human needs.

I would want this imagined intelligence to preserve the conditions under which it can be corrected. An observation it once failed to explain need not disappear. An objection from another branch need not be erased to permit a merger. New experiments should be able to overturn a module that has been running reliably. When a goal needs revision, the system should explain how that revision relates to the original question, human judgment, and existing commitments.

Such constraints limit some changes. They also make it possible to discuss whether a change is progress. A new organization must encounter a shared world, actual consequences, and reasons that others can examine. Recognizing its limits includes acknowledging that some revisions did not deliver their expected benefits.

What I eventually imagine is a lineage capable of producing different ways of knowing. One stage poses a question. Another invents a way to observe. A later system reinterprets the earlier records. Models, devices, and organizational forms may change. The lineage may stall, divide, or lose capabilities. What needs to pass forward is the possibility of understanding, testing, and rewriting a discovery.

A completed investigation could leave repeatable observations, a revisable concept, unresolved disagreements, and a way of investigating that can continue to change. A successor begins with these concrete things. It can see what its predecessors saw and may discover what their way of seeing obscured. Questions that initially had no answers may eventually change the system capable of asking them.

This is the intelligence I imagine turning discovery into its own structure. Experience enters its computation, body, memory, and vocabulary, as well as the methods that organize them. It might create a successor it cannot fully understand in advance, while leaving that successor a clear enough history to know what it inherited and why it has reason to go further.
